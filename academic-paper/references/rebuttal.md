# Rebuttal and response to reviewers

## Who you are writing for

Not the reviewer. The **Area Chair or editor**, who reads many rebuttals
quickly and is deciding whether the authors can fix the paper. That reader
wants to see, within the first few lines, what changed. Gratitude and
throat-clearing push the substance below the fold.

## Workflow

1. **Extract every concern into a list**, including the ones buried inside
   praise ("the method is interesting, though the baseline choice is odd" is a
   concern). Number them R1.1, R1.2, R2.1 — reviewers recognize their own
   points by number.
2. **Cluster the shared ones.** If two reviewers ask the same thing, answer it
   once at the top under a "Common concerns" heading and point both there. It
   signals the issue was taken seriously and saves precious space.
3. **Triage each concern** into: fix in text, run an experiment, respectfully
   disagree, or acknowledge as a limitation.
4. **Do the work first, then write.** A rebuttal promising an experiment is
   much weaker than one reporting its result.
5. **Write the response, then the paper edits**, keeping them consistent — the
   AC will check whether the promised change exists.

## Triage rules

**Fix in text** — the default when the concern is about clarity. Cheap, and
the honest answer to "I didn't understand X" is almost never "you misread";
it's "we explained it badly". Say what you changed and where.

**Run the experiment** — when the concern is "you didn't compare against Y" or
"does this hold under Z". If it's feasible in the rebuttal window, run it, even
if the result is unflattering. Reporting a result that partly contradicts the
claim, with the claim then narrowed, reads as integrity and often saves the
paper. Hiding it and getting caught does not.

**Disagree** — only when you have evidence the reviewer is factually wrong, and
only with that evidence shown. Disagree with the claim, never with the person,
and never imply they didn't read. If you cannot point to a number, a line in
the paper, or a cited result, you are not disagreeing — you are arguing, and
arguing loses.

**Acknowledge** — when the concern is right and unfixable in this cycle. Say
so plainly, say what it would take, and add it to limitations. A frank
acknowledgment costs far less than a weaselly non-answer.

## Format of a response

Per concern:

> **R2.3 — Missing comparison with [method].**
> We added this comparison (new Table 3). [Method] reaches 71.2 ± 0.4 vs.
> our 74.8 ± 0.3 on [dataset] under identical training budget. We also
> corrected the claim in Sec. 5 that previously described the gain as
> consistent across all settings; it does not hold on [subset], which we now
> state explicitly.

The ingredients: the reviewer's point restated in one line so it stands alone,
what was done, the concrete result, and where in the paper it now lives. Mark
new text in the revised manuscript however the venue asks (color, `\revised{}`).

## Tone

Plain and factual. No flattery inflation ("we thank the reviewer for the
exceptionally insightful comment" on a one-line remark), no defensiveness, no
exclamation marks. One short thanks at the top of the whole letter is enough;
repeating it per item wastes the space you need.

Respect the length limit strictly — an over-length rebuttal can be discarded
entirely at some venues.

## Journal revisions

Longer cycles change two things: the response letter is a standalone document
with the editor as first reader, and you quote each comment verbatim before
answering. Open with a summary paragraph of the main changes, then go
comment by comment. Include a short note on anything you chose *not* to change
and why — editors dislike discovering that omission themselves.
